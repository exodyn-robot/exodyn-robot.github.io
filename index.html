ExoDyn: Decoupling Visuo- and -Motor Learning for Hyper-Dynamic Manipulation

Zhenyang Feng, Jimin Heo, Erik Sudderth, Unnat Jain
University of California, Irvine
Paper (coming soon) Code (coming soon)

Abstract

Humans learn reactive skills like catching a ball in two parts: they first learn how a thrown ball flies, then practice moving their own hands to meet it. Getting robots to succeed at these tasks requires a similar process. However, manipulation is usually trained as one visuo-motor skill, by imitating teleoperated demonstrations. In hyper-dynamic tasks, the object moves at several meters per second, and the robot has a fraction of a second to react. A teleoperator cannot keep up, and the demonstrations miss much of the skill.

We instead decouple visuo-motor learning, as people do. Until contact, the object's motion does not depend on the robot, so the robot learns these scene dynamics by watching. The robot then learns its actor dynamics by practice, with reinforcement learning in a physics simulator. Without teleoperated demonstrations, our ExoDyn policy catches 65% of balls thrown from 6 meters away, blocks 80% of lightsaber swings, and completes 94% of handovers from a walking person, succeeding more often than a human teleoperator on the first two. Vision-language-action models fall far behind on all three skills, and baseline RL policies trained on hand-designed physical parameters catch less than half as many balls.

Too Fast to Demonstrate: Try It Yourself

Manipulation policies are usually taught by imitating a person who teleoperates the robot. In hyper-dynamic tasks, that teacher cannot keep up. Try it yourself: each throw below is a real recording from the robot's head camera. Grab the catcher plate and drag it to catch the ball.

This mock teleoperation is easier than the real one. The plate follows your pointer with no delay, while our human expert steered the arm through a leader arm, and the teleoperation system added a delay of its own. Even so, the ball arrives about 0.6 s after it leaves the thrower's hand. In the real world, the human expert catches 37.5% of throws and misses most of the high ones, and the vision-language-action models trained on these demonstrations catch at most 16%.

ExoDyn Methodology

ExoDyn learns a reactive skill in two parts that share one variable, the object's intended motion: the path the object would take if the robot were not there. Scene dynamics, the distribution of intended motions, are learned in the real world from recordings made with the robot powered off (top left). Actor dynamics, how the robot should move in response, are learned with reinforcement learning in a physics simulator (top right). The trained policy is then copied to the robot, frozen, and run on the detections of the real camera (bottom). The four parts below show each step.

1. Exogenous Motion Modeling

Tasks:
RealSim

Sim-to-real reinforcement learning usually designs the object's motion by hand. ExoDyn learns it from the real world instead. Until contact, the object's motion is exogenous: it does not depend on the robot. So it can be learned just by watching, with the robot powered off. A person who holds the object is masked or blindfolded, so their motion does not depend on the robot either. Drag the divider to compare a recording with the same motion in simulation.

2. Generating Scene Dynamics

Tasks:

Reinforcement learning needs far more episodes than a few minutes of recordings hold. So ExoDyn trains a motion diffusion model on the recordings to learn the scene dynamics, the distribution of how the object moves, and draws a new motion from it for every training episode. It turns 200 recorded throws into 100,000, and 5 minutes of lightsaber swings into 13 hours. Our deployed lightsaber policy never trained on a recorded swing, yet it blocks 80% of real attacks. The clips show the model's denoising steps.

3. Soft Replay: Physics-Grounded Motion

0.5× Faint blade: intended motion

To learn to block, the robot must practice against swings that its blade can actually stop. ExoDyn treats each generated motion as an intent, not a fixed path. In ExoDyn's simulator, soft replay moves the lightsaber along the generated motion until it touches the robot. After contact, it keeps trying to follow that motion, but physics constrains it, so a blocked swing stops at the robot's blade and slides along it. The robot thus practices against swings learned from people, with contact that obeys physics.

4. Massively Parallel RL, Zero-Shot Transfer

The robot then practices against generated motion with reinforcement learning, thousands of simulated robots at a time. The policy acts only on what the real robot also has: its joint state, its last action, and a short history of object detections from its head camera. In simulation, these detections get the noise, delay, and missed frames of the real detector. The detection history tells the policy where the object is heading, so no separate trajectory predictor is needed. After about 4 hours of training on one GPU, the policy is copied to the robot, frozen, and runs in the real world zero-shot.

Analysis

Consistent Success on Far Throws and Fast Swings

ExoDyn maintains a consistent success rate as balls arrive farther from the plate's ready position (left). On throws that arrive more than 41 cm away, no imitation or sim-to-real baseline makes a single catch, and ExoDyn still does. On Lightsaber Defense, ExoDyn blocks about 80% of attacks at every tip speed of the opponent's blade (right), while the baselines either do poorly at all speeds or decline as the swings get faster.

Emergent Behaviors

ExoDyn tilts the plate toward each throw and corrects its position as the ball comes in. Times are counted from the release.

ExoDyn holds a horizontal guard and defends each strike with minimal movement. Times are counted from the start of the swing.

Nobody taught the policy these behaviors; they emerge from practice in simulation. In Ball Catch, the robot turns the plate to face fast throws and corrects its reach mid-flight as new detections arrive. In Lightsaber Defense, it holds a horizontal guard before the opponent attacks, so an overhead strike needs little extra movement. It deflects the opponent's blade with a small push instead of a large swing: its blade turns 55.9°/s on average against the opponent's 91.8°/s, yet it blocks 80.1% of attacks.

Why the Baselines Fall Short

Imitation is limited by its teacher and by its learner. The human expert's demonstrations miss many of the hardest throws, and the policies trained on them catch less than half as many balls as the expert. Better demonstrations alone do not close the gap: π0.5 trained on 45 of ExoDyn's own successful catches, which include throws the expert misses, still catches only 12.0% of throws, about the same as with the teleoperated demonstrations (11.0%).

Sim-to-real is limited by hand-designed motion. A simulator can be wrong in two places: in how the object moves (the scene gap), and in the robot, its sensing, and the contact (the actor gap). The sim-to-real baselines lose most of their simulated success in the real world, and identifying a physical parameter does not help: on Ball Catch, Phys2Real loses even more than Dynamics Randomization. They fail most where a person moves the object for the whole episode, since no physical law predicts that motion. ExoDyn learns the object's motion from recordings and keeps its success. On Lightsaber Defense and Bottle Handover it even does better in the real world, because the generated motion is more varied than what the person performs in our evaluation.
